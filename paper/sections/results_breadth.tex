Zero-shot, the decision models lead by wide margins (Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot}). In the pre-registered comparison
against \gj{} with the clean NLI member, \jev{} is ahead on every condition (Holm over four primary conditions:
Banking77 and MTOP, $p_\text{Holm}\le\fsZsBankingDescJevPHolm$; Holm over six further conditions on CLINC150, SNIPS
and Bitext, $p_\text{Holm}\le\fsZsSnipsDescJevPHolm$). \pplx{} is higher than \jev{} on every condition where both were run (with different request wording), and \clef{} reaches
\zxClincNameClefBigAcc\% on CLINC150 with label names (the only completed \clef{} zero-shot cell).

How to read these numbers is the point of this subsection. Only one column is interpretable as zero-shot for every
dataset: \gj{} with the clean NLI member, whose three members document their training data and exclude all seven
benchmarks. The other columns mix three cases. Some cells are \emph{exposed}: \pplx's training manifest lists
MASSIVE, MTOP and SNIPS, and our own original NLI member inherits the Banking77 and MASSIVE training splits through
its initialisation (\S\ref{sec:find-exposure}). Some are \emph{undisclosed}: \jev{} and \clef{} do not document their
training data, and the remaining \pplx{} cells are not covered by its manifest. Some are \emph{indirect}: HWU64 and
MASSIVE share a large fraction of their utterances. We therefore report decision-model zero-shot numbers as
measurements of these systems on these test sets, not as evidence of zero-shot generalisation.

Within \gj, the internal findings that motivated it hold with the clean member: the jury beats its best single
member on \zxCleanNliWins{} of 14 conditions (Holm), including all \zxCleanNliUnexposedOf{} conditions on datasets that
are unexposed for every member, and loses on \zxCleanNliLosses. Appendix~\ref{app:jury} reports the jury in full.

To test whether the Banking77 picture holds on a second dataset, we pre-registered a 24-shot comparison of all five
systems on Bitext customer support \citep{bitext-2023-customer-support}. Bitext has no official test split, so the
test set is our own stratified \btTestPct\% split (\btN{} items, \btK{} intents); a fresh selection split of
\btSelN{} utterances was held out for choosing the \orb{} configuration, and the remaining \btPoolN{} utterances form
the retrieval pool. The 24 examples for each test item were retrieved once with BM25 (ties broken by pool position)
and recorded, so every system saw the same examples in the same order. All \btN{} decision-model bodies kept all 24
examples and were checked against a hash manifest before they were sent; the longest prompt (\btMaxPromptTokens{}
tokens) is far below every model's length limit, and no item was refused, failed or left unsent. On the selection
split, \btSelTied{} of the \btCandidates{} \orb{} candidates were tied at a perfect score, and the pre-declared
tie-break chose the simplest of them (reader and nearest-neighbour vote, label names, one example order). Each
system was scored once, and the analysis script was committed before any result was read.

\paragraph{The dataset is close to its ceiling.} A text-only audit of the split, run before any model was scored,
found \btExactDup{} test items whose normalised text also occurs in the pool and \btNearDup{} (\btNearDupPct\%) with
a TF-IDF cosine of at least 0.8 to a pool utterance. % numbers-ok: the pre-registered threshold
Copying the label of the top retrieved example is already correct on \btFloorAcc\% of the test set. Because
full-set differences could therefore rest on very few items, we added a secondary analysis, fixed before any result
was read, on the \btNSub{} test items with neither kind of duplicate.

\paragraph{Results.} Every system reaches at least \btFlashSubAcc\% on both sets (Table~\ref{tab:bitext}); the five
systems differ by at most \btFlashErr{} errors in \btN. Four of the ten pairwise tests are significant after Holm
correction, the same four in both sets: \jev{} and \pplx{} are each more accurate than both \clef{} models (full set, b/c and
$p_\text{Holm}$: \jev{} vs.\ \clef{} $\btJevVsClefB/\btJevVsClefC$, $\btJevVsClefPHolm$; \jev{} vs.\ \flash{}
$\btJevVsFlashB/\btJevVsFlashC$, $\btJevVsFlashPHolm$; \pplx{} vs.\ \clef{} $\btClefVsPplxC/\btClefVsPplxB$,
$\btClefVsPplxPHolm$; \pplx{} vs.\ \flash{} $\btFlashVsPplxC/\btFlashVsPplxB$, $\btFlashVsPplxPHolm$). \orb{} cannot be separated from any decision model
(all $p_\text{Holm}\ge\btOpenVsPplxPHolm$). The ordering of the decision models is dataset-specific: on Banking77
both \clef{} models were more accurate than \jev{} on identical bodies (\S\ref{sec:res-calibration}), and on Bitext
\jev{} is more accurate than both. On calibration, against \orb, \clef{}
and \flash{} have higher ECE (differences \btClefEceDiff{} and \btFlashEceDiff, CIs [\btClefEceDiffLo,
\btClefEceDiffHi] and [\btFlashEceDiffLo, \btFlashEceDiffHi]) and higher Brier scores; \pplx{} has a lower Brier
score (\btPplxBrierDiff, CI [\btPplxBrierDiffLo, \btPplxBrierDiffHi]); \jev's ECE is slightly higher on the full set
(\btJevEceDiff, CI [\btJevEceDiffLo, \btJevEceDiffHi]) but not on the subset.

\paragraph{What this dataset can show.} Bitext adds a second dataset on which the protocol separates the decision
models from one another, but near the ceiling it cannot separate our baseline from them. It also carries exposure
we cannot remove. The test split is ours, not the dataset's. \jev{} received these test texts, without labels, in
our zero-shot run (\S\ref{sec:res-zeroshot}). The one-line label descriptions used as criteria in every decision-model
body were written before this study and had already been evaluated zero-shot on this split; they were not revised.
The selected \orb{} configuration uses label names and not these descriptions, and our reader's adapter was not
trained on Bitext. \clef's and \pplx's training data
do not document Bitext (Table~\ref{tab:exposure}). Finally, after the analysis script was committed, the operator
saw the headline summaries of two arms before the joint analysis run; nothing was changed afterwards, and the run log
records both events.

\subsection{Beyond intent: thirteen code and workflow decision tasks}
\label{sec:res-13task}

The protocol is not specific to intent classification. We applied it, unchanged in its rules, to a pre-registered
model-selection study among the three open decision models on \dtTasks{} decision tasks (\dtTotalItems{} scored
decisions), choosing which model to adopt for code and workflow decisions: nine software-engineering decisions on
source code and bug reports (patch triage, duplicate bug reports, program equivalence, clone detection,
vulnerability detection, and a synthetic vulnerability floor) and four business-workflow evaluations scored by
their own harness (Table~\ref{tab:d13}). Every model received the same request bodies, checked
by hash, on one GPU, through serving engines that had passed (or, for one model, narrowly failed and were labelled;
\S\ref{sec:find-serving}) the parity gate. The decision rule, fixed before any call, counts each model's
Holm-significant pairwise wins minus losses on accuracy (\dtTestsE{} tests for the code tasks, \dtTestsC{} for the
workflows). \jev{} was not part of this study. The code sets are PatchNet \citep{hoang-etal-2021-patchnet},
TOSEM-DBRD \citep{zhang-etal-2023-dbrd}, EquiBench \citep{wei-etal-2025-equibench}, POJ-104 % numbers-ok: dataset name
\citep{mou-etal-2016-tbcnn} in its CodeXGLUE release \citep{lu-etal-2021-codexglue}, PrimeVul
\citep{ding-etal-2025-primevul}, SV-TrustEval-C \citep{li-etal-2025-svtrusteval}, SecVulEval
\citep{ahmed-etal-2025-secvuleval} and the Juliet C/C++ suite \citep{nist-2017-juliet}; the paraphrase control is PAWS
\citep{zhang-etal-2019-paws}; the workflows are WorkflowEvals tasks run with their own harness
\citep{typesafe-2026-workflowevals,typesafe-2026-evalsafe-invoice}.

Under the pre-registered rule \pplx{} ranks first ($\dtScorePplx$), \clef{} second ($\dtScoreClef$) and \flash{} third
($\dtScoreFlash$), and the order holds without the two exposed tasks ($\dtScoreNoExpPplx$, $\dtScoreNoExpClef$,
$\dtScoreNoExpFlash$). The aggregate hides a varied picture. No model is best everywhere: \clef{} is the only
significant winner on program equivalence, and both \clef{} models beat \pplx{} on security incidents, while
\pplx{} is significantly better than both others on \dtBestEPplx{} of the nine code tasks. Accuracy and calibration again rank the models differently:
on the pre-declared calibration tie-break (Holm-significant ECE wins minus losses over the \dtEceTests{} tests on
tasks whose per-item probabilities are public), \flash{}, last on accuracy, scores $\dtEceScoreFlash$, \clef{}
$\dtEceScoreClef$ and \pplx{} $\dtEceScorePplx$. The paraphrase control, whose training split is part of the data
builder released with \pplx{} (its test split, which we score, is held out there;
\citealp{perplexity-2026-pplx-decider-card}), shows the expected exposure gap (\dtPawsPplxAcc\% vs.\ \dtPawsClefAcc\% and \dtPawsFlashAcc\%) and is
kept out of the decision rule. Two results in the table are not about decision quality at all: they are decided by
how each model handles inputs longer than its limit (\S\ref{sec:find-serving}).

Per-item outputs (identifiers, labels and model outputs, no item text) are released for the nine tasks whose
licences allow it; for one code task and three workflows that carry no data licence we release only the per-pair
discordant counts, from which every accuracy test above is recomputed exactly. The calibration tie-break therefore
covers the tasks with public per-item data only. The synthetic floor carries a known structural cue (benign
variants are longer and have more functions) and never decides the ranking on its own.
