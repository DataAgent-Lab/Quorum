\subsection{Replicating the closed API}
\label{sec:res-replication}
We re-sent the reproduction's \fsN{} request bodies, unchanged, to the pinned \jev{} version. Accuracy was
\fsJevAAcc\%, equal to the published \fsJevPubAcc\%; the two runs agree on \fsJevAAgreement\% of choices and differ
in correctness on $\fsJevAVsJevB+\fsJevAVsJevC$ items, evenly split ($p=\fsJevAVsJevP$). Our recomputation of the
published calibration from its per-item probabilities also matches the reported values (ECE \fsJevPubEce, Brier
\fsJevPubBrier). Every later \jev{} comparison is therefore against a reference we can reproduce.

\subsection{The retrieval rule moves results as much as the model}
\label{sec:res-retrieval}
Our baseline \orb{} exceeds the published \jev{} result:
\fsCleanAcc\% vs.\ \fsJevPubAcc\% (b/c $\fsCleanVsJevB/\fsCleanVsJevC$, $p=\fsCleanVsJevP$; pre-registered,
single test run). The protocol shows that this advantage belongs to the evidence, not to the model:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Same evidence, tie.} Sending \jev{} the 24 examples that \orb{} retrieved raises it to
  \fsJevBAcc\%; against \orb{} on these identical bodies the difference is $\fsCleanMinusJevBPt$ points
  (b/c $\fsCleanVsJevBB/\fsCleanVsJevBC$, $p=\fsCleanVsJevBP$). This comparison was the pre-registered primary
  analysis of the swap.
  \item \textbf{The retrieval rule alone moves \jev.} With our retrieval instead of the reproduction's, \jev{} gains
  \fsJevBMinusJevAPt{} points (b/c $\fsJevBVsJevAB/\fsJevBVsJevAC$, $p=\fsJevBVsJevAP$).
  \item \textbf{The reverse also holds.} \orb{} scored with the reproduction's retrieval rule reaches
  \fsParBestAcc\%, a tie with \jev{} (b/c $\fsParBestVsJevB/\fsParBestVsJevC$, $p=\fsParBestVsJevP$); the same model
  with its own retrieval is \fsCleanMinusParBestPt{} points higher (b/c $\fsCleanVsParBestB/\fsCleanVsParBestC$,
  $p=\fsCleanVsParBestP$).
  \item \textbf{The open decision models gain little or nothing.} With our retrieval instead of the
  reproduction's, accuracy changes by $\fsPplxBMinusPplxPt$ points for \pplx{} (b/c
  $\fsPplxBVsPplxB/\fsPplxBVsPplxC$, $p=\fsPplxBVsPplxP$), $\fsClefBMinusClefPt$ for \clef{} (b/c
  $\fsClefBVsClefB/\fsClefBVsClefC$, $p=\fsClefBVsClefP$) and $\fsFlashBMinusFlashPt$ for \flash{} (b/c
  $\fsFlashBVsFlashB/\fsFlashBVsFlashC$, $p=\fsFlashBVsFlashP$). None of the three changes is significant, unlike
  the gains of \jev{} and \orb{} above. These comparisons were pre-registered as secondary and are not corrected
  for multiplicity. A non-significant change does not show that the effect is zero, only that it is too small to
  detect on this test set.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What in the rule matters (post hoc).} The two retrieval rules differ in three ways: a per-class cap of
four examples, bigram tokens, and the pool (full training split vs.\ our pool without the selection split). A
factorial re-run of \orb{} on our pool (Holm over six contrasts) attributes the gain mainly to the cap. Removing it
raises accuracy from \fsCapUniAcc\% to \fsCleanAcc\% with unigram tokens (b/c $\fsCapEffUniB/\fsCapEffUniC$,
$p_\text{Holm}=\fsCapEffUniPHolm$); tokenisation contributes a borderline part (b/c $\fsBiEffNocapB/\fsBiEffNocapC$,
$p_\text{Holm}=\fsBiEffNocapPHolm$); the pool has no effect (b/c $\fsPoolEffB/\fsPoolEffC$). Without the cap the
retriever returns fewer distinct classes per query (\fsCleanClasses{} vs.\ \fsCapUniClasses), i.e.\ more
neighbours from the most likely class. Because this analysis is post hoc and covers one dataset, we report it as a
mechanism to test, not as a general rule.

\paragraph{What in \orb{} matters (post hoc).} Most of \orb's accuracy comes from retrieval and the nearest-neighbour
vote: the vote alone reaches \fsKnnAloneAcc\%, itself a tie with the published \jev{} result
($p=\fsKnnAloneVsJevP$). The reader adds a small, significant increment over the vote alone
(\fsCleanMinusKnnAlonePt{} points; b/c $\fsCleanVsKnnAloneB/\fsCleanVsKnnAloneC$,
$p_\text{Holm}=\fsCleanVsKnnAlonePHolm$), but the reader's task adapter does not add a significant increment once
the vote is present (base reader with the vote: \fsBaseKnnAcc\%, $p_\text{Holm}=\fsCleanVsBaseKnnPHolm$). The
reader alone scores \fsReaderAloneAcc\%, below \jev{} though not significantly ($p=\fsReaderAloneVsJevP$).
A standard fine-tune on the same pool reaches \fsFtAcc\%, a tie with \orb{} ($p=\fsCleanVsFtP$).

\subsection{Accuracy and calibration rank models differently}
\label{sec:res-calibration}
On the reproduction's bodies, the accuracy order is \flash{} > \clef{} $\approx$ \pplx{} > \jev{} $\approx$ \orb{}
(\flash{} vs.\ \jev: b/c $\fsFlashVsJevAB/\fsFlashVsJevAC$, $p=\fsFlashVsJevAP$; \clef{} vs.\ \pplx:
$p=\fsPplxVsClefP$; \orb{} vs.\ \jev: $p=\fsParBestVsJevP$). Calibration orders them differently
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). \flash{}, the most accurate, has the highest ECE (\fsFlashEce) and a higher log loss than
\orb{} on identical evidence (difference \fsLlParMinusFlash, 95\% CI % numbers-ok: confidence level
 [\fsLlParMinusFlashLo, \fsLlParMinusFlashHi]).

On our retrieval's bodies, all three open decision models are more accurate than both \orb{} and \jev{}. Against
\orb, the b/c counts are $\fsFlashBVsCleanB/\fsFlashBVsCleanC$ for \flash{} ($p=\fsFlashBVsCleanP$),
$\fsClefBVsCleanB/\fsClefBVsCleanC$ for \clef{} ($p=\fsClefBVsCleanP$) and $\fsPplxBVsCleanB/\fsPplxBVsCleanC$ for
\pplx{} ($p=\fsPplxBVsCleanP$); against \jev{} they are $\fsFlashBVsJevBB/\fsFlashBVsJevBC$, $\fsClefBVsJevBB/\fsClefBVsJevBC$
and $\fsPplxBVsJevBB/\fsPplxBVsJevBC$ ($p\le\fsClefBVsJevBP$). These comparisons were pre-registered as secondary and are
not corrected for multiplicity. The three models cannot be separated from one another (post hoc pairwise tests, all
$p\ge\fsPplxBVsFlashBP$).
The significant lead of \flash{} over \pplx{} on the reproduction's bodies (b/c $\fsPplxVsFlashC/\fsPplxVsFlashB$,
$p=\fsPplxVsFlashP$) does not carry over to ours (b/c $\fsPplxBVsFlashBC/\fsPplxBVsFlashBB$, $p=\fsPplxBVsFlashBP$).
The calibration pattern is unchanged: \flash{} again has the highest ECE (\fsFlashBEce) and a higher log loss than
\orb{} (difference \fsLlCleanMinusFlashB, CI [\fsLlCleanMinusFlashBLo, \fsLlCleanMinusFlashBHi]), while \clef's log
loss is not distinguishable from \orb's (\fsLlCleanMinusClefB, CI [\fsLlCleanMinusClefBLo, \fsLlCleanMinusClefBHi]).
\pplx{} matches the lowest ECE and has the lowest Brier score in both blocks. Against \jev{} on identical evidence,
\orb{} has lower ECE (difference \fsSwapDiffEce, CI [\fsSwapDiffEceLo, \fsSwapDiffEceHi]) and a Brier score that is
not distinguishable (\fsSwapDiffBrier, CI [\fsSwapDiffBrierLo, \fsSwapDiffBrierHi]). A system's accuracy rank is
therefore not a proxy for the quality of its probabilities, which matters for decision models whose stated purpose
is to return usable probabilities.
